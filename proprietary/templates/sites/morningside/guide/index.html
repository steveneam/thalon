How this page was made

Morningside is a fictional business. It does not exist, you cannot buy anything from it, and the three farms, the prices and the roast schedule are written to demonstrate a page, not to describe a company. Every photograph on it is AI-generated. This note says exactly how, because a page that argues for honesty about coffee should not be quietly dishonest about itself.

The idea

Almost every scroll-driven page treats its animation as a chapter: you scroll, something performs, you carry on. Here the animation is the page. One ceramic cup holds the middle of the screen from the first screen to the last, and its contents change from whole beans to ground coffee to a poured cup as you read. The writing hangs off those three states — the origin section belongs to the beans, the freshness argument belongs to the grounds, the payoff belongs to the pour.

That was chosen before anything was built, and it set a high floor: with the motion carrying the whole page there is no instrument, no chart and no diagram to fall back on. Coffee has excellent instruments — a roast curve, a brew-ratio table, an extraction chart — and this page deliberately ships none of them, because a page for someone who just wants the cup should not open with a control chart.

Why the hero is a frame and not a photograph

Each video segment was directed by still "keyframes" — an image of the beans, an image of the grounds — handed to the model as the take's start and end. Those keyframes are directing instruments, not shipping assets: measured, the take does not start exactly on the still it was given. Its first frame sat about 7 units of average brightness away from the keyframe that produced it, because the model recomposes slightly. So the hero image at the top of the page is frame 0 of the take, not the still that directed it. It is the same picture the sequence begins on, which is the only way the hero and the first scrubbed frame can be the same thing.

The seam between the two takes

Two segments means one join. It was made by taking segment one's actual last frame and handing that to the model as segment two's start, so the two takes share a frame by construction rather than by luck. Measured, that bought an exactly matching geometry — the camera did not move and the frame did not recompose, offset zero pixels in both axes — and not a matching exposure: segment two came back about five units darker.

Measured per frame rather than once, that darkness turned out not to be a constant re-grade of the second take at all. It is a settling transient at the head of every take: segment two opens five units dark and recovers within about sixteen frames, and segment one does the same thing over its first eighty. Correcting the seam with a single constant — the obvious fix — would have levelled the join and over-brightened the whole poured cup at the end by about six units.

So every frame of both takes is levelled individually onto one reference exposure, measured on a patch of the linen that does not move. That is arithmetic on our side, it costs nothing, and it fixes the join, the opening of each take, and the hero still at once. No frame on this page is more than one unit off that reference.

How many frames, and why that many

Frame count is not a house number, it is a measurement. Each take was decimated to several candidate counts and the average difference between neighbouring frames compared against a sequence known to read smoothly. At 41 frames each take lands under that benchmark, so both ship at 41 — 82 frames for the whole page. A more turbulent subject needs twice that; a cup of settling grounds does not.

What happens without JavaScript

All 82 frames are real markup, stacked, with exactly one of each sequence marked as lit. With scripting off you get the page's first state as a still image, every chapter of the writing, the lot panel and the closing photograph — everything except the scrubbing itself. With reduced motion requested, the sequence does not interpolate: it steps between the four chapter states instead, so the three states are all still shown and nothing moves continuously under you.

Type and colour

Two families, both open-licensed and served from this site rather than a font CDN: Piazzolla for everything the brand says in its own voice and Onest for everything it states as fact. There is deliberately no monospaced face anywhere, because a monospace reads as instrumentation and this page is a product page, not an instrument.

The palette was read off the photograph rather than chosen against it: the linen, the warm shadow the raking light leaves, and one amber that appears only where coffee appears.

What is honest here

  • The business is invented. So are the farms, the altitudes and the prices.
  • Every image is generated. None is a photograph of real coffee.
  • The page makes no claim about grind size or brew method, on purpose: the generated grounds render finer than the page would need to claim, and writing copy the picture contradicts is the one thing an image-led page must not do.
  • There is no countdown, no "only three left", and no testimonial. Nothing on the page is designed to hurry you.
  • The credits, models and prompts behind each asset are recorded with the asset, not summarised from memory.

Build log

Three full passes, each running both a fault-hunt lane and an ambition lane. Every fault below was found by driving the page in a browser and measuring it — none of them were visible in the markup, and none would have failed a screenshot.

PassWhat the fault lane caughtWhat the ambition lane added
OneThe hero covered a near-square window with a wide photograph, cropping the cup down to a speck and putting the headline across it. And the scroll was moving 17 pixels per frame through the grind — a single wheel notch skipping five or six frames of the two beats this page exists for. Chapter lengths are now derived from how many frames each one drives. The page's one accent warms from roasted brown to amber along the same clock as the spine, so its only colour arrives as the coffee does.
TwoThe narrow-screen rules re-declared the chapter padding as a shorthand, which silently discarded the pacing calculation: 31 of the 82 frames were unreachable on a phone while the desktop layout was perfect. The same rules reset the stage's position but not its height, so the sticky column was taller than the window and the pin was simply dead on phones. A woven grain behind the whole page, so the linen the photographs are shot on carries onto the paper they sit on.
ThreeThe accent failed contrast at the end of its own arc — 4.11:1 against the paper, under the 4.5 minimum, on the state readout and the order button. Only wrong once fully warmed, which is why a static check misses it. And the closing photograph, bled to the window edge, arrived out of a bright page as 200 pixels of near-black wall. The four marks under the picture became four real links, so a very long page can be navigated and returned to rather than only scrolled.
