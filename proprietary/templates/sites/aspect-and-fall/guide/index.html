Aspect & Fall · method note

How this page was made, and what in it is real.

First, the disclosure

Aspect & Fall is not a real company. It is a fictional garden and landscape design studio, invented as a demonstration of what this build process produces. There is no studio, no client, and no garden at that address — the enquiry address is a reserved example domain and reaches nobody.

Every photograph on the page is AI-generated. None of them is a photograph of a real place. They were generated for this page, and the exact model, prompt and cost of each is listed below.

There are no invented testimonials, awards, client names or project counts anywhere on the page. “Two of us, twelve gardens a year” is a description of the fictional studio's scale and nothing more.

What is real

The botany. Every species named on the plan and in the interest calendar is a real plant, and its flowering and interest months are its real ones — Helleborus argutifolius in January to March, Narcissus ‘Thalia’ in March and April, Salvia nemorosa ‘Caradonna’ from late May, Molinia caerulea ‘Transparent’ carrying seed heads into November, Betula utilis var. jacquemontii earning its place entirely in winter. A page arguing that a drawing tells the truth about months would be a poor joke if the months were made up.

The drawing conventions are real too: plan view, canopy circles at mature spread, leader lines to letterspaced labels, contours and spot levels on the survey, a north point, a scale, and a numbered sheet set. That is what a landscape practice actually issues.

The numbers in the title block — how many species are in flower, the percentage of the garden under canopy shade, the hour the sun comes off the terrace — are computed from the same plant model that draws the plan, on the same scroll clock. They are consistent with the drawing rather than typed in beside it. They describe a garden that does not exist, but they are not decorative.

The instrument, and why it is a diagram

The page has one moving part: a calendar year, driven by scrolling. It sets the coloured-pencil fill in the beds, which species are lit in their own colour, the shade the canopy throws across the terrace, the three readouts, the playhead crossing the interest calendar, and how far the photograph of the garden corner has moved through its own year.

The original idea for this page was a naturalistic tree drawn in code, turning through four seasons. It was rejected before a line of it was drawn, on a test this factory learned the expensive way on an earlier site: does the real discipline produce this drawing? A hand-drawn tree is a picture of a thing, and pictures of things do not converge in code — they read as illustration. A plan-view planting drawing is not a picture of a garden; it is the document a landscape architect actually hands over. So the instrument became that, and the photographs carry the feeling instead. The division of labour on every page here is the same: the photographs carry the feeling, the diagram carries the evidence.

The seasons are one place, and now they move

The photograph on the sheet is deliberately one corner: the same paving, the same wall, the same bench, the same birches. Four keyframes were generated first — July, then February, May and October edited out of it — and the page then moves between them. Scrolling does not cut from one photograph to the next; it scrubs three generated transitions, February to May to July to October, in which the camera never moves and only the planting changes.

The transitions are generated video, but the page never plays a video. Each one is decoded to a sequence of stills and the scroll position picks the frame, because seeking a compressed video file lands on its keyframes and stutters. Outside February to October the sequence holds at its nearest end: that is the extent of the record, and it is not padded out.

Three takes were rejected on the way, and the rejections are the honest kind worth recording. The first February keyframe drifted to a different viewpoint, which would have quietly turned “the same corner” into a false caption. The first May keyframe came back with the hydrangea in full flower — botanically wrong for May, and in direct contradiction of this page's own interest calendar. And the October keyframe, which shipped in the first version of this page, turned out to be a different camera: lower, further back, and lit by a hard backlight the other three do not have. A cross-fade had been hiding it. Scrubbing between frames does not hide anything, so the fault became obvious the moment the page started to move, and October was regenerated to match. Two of the three defects on this page were caught by making it move.

Every image was minted on a paid tier and pinned to content-addressed storage with its full provenance record — model, prompt, parameters, credits, licence tier and timestamp — at the moment it was made. The page references no vendor URL and loads nothing from a third-party host.

How it is built

  • One self-contained index.html: no framework, no build step, no external requests of any kind.
  • Two typefaces, both open-licensed and served from this directory — Spectral for reading and for the Latin names, Sometype Mono for every piece of drawing-sheet apparatus. A real sheet has two lettering registers; so does this page.
  • The plan, the survey, the interest calendar and the three maturity diagrams are SVG, written into the page itself rather than drawn by script — the script only moves what is already there. They cost nothing to generate and they can respond to the visitor, which a photograph cannot. That is the division: the drawings are computed and true, the photograph is generated and honest about it.
  • The one colour rule: saturated colour belongs to plants only, and it changes with the month. Nothing decorative on this page is coloured.
  • Reduced motion freezes the clock at high summer, shows every chapter at once, and never builds the frame sequence at all — a reader who asked for less motion is served the single static July photograph. With JavaScript off, every chapter, the whole interest calendar and the plan are all still there in the markup — including the note on the terrace — and the photograph is that same static July frame. The scrubbed transition is an enhancement layered on top; none of its frames are in the document until the page decides to move.

A demonstration page. The studio is fictional; the plants are not.
