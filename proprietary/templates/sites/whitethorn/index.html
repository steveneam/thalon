One dog, eighty-four days.

DAY 0

The limp you had stopped noticing

His owner booked for something else entirely. The shortened stride had come on over about four months, which is slow enough that a household adjusts to it without ever deciding to.

lameness 4/5 · symmetry 61% · weight on limb 38%

DAY 1

Walking him, not examining him

A lameness exam starts on the ground — at a walk, then a trot, from behind and from the side. Most of a diagnosis is in which beat of the cycle comes up short, and you cannot hear that from a consulting table.

lameness 4/5 · symmetry 62% · weight on limb 39%

DAY 6

What the images showed

Cruciate disease in the right stifle, with the wear pattern of a joint that had been compensated for a long time. The other hind leg was carrying too much and had started to say so.

lameness 4/5 · symmetry 64% · weight on limb 41%

DAY 12

The repair — and it gets worse first

Surgery is a day. For the fortnight after it he is worse than he came in, which is normal and which we tell you before you agree to it. The numbers beside this dip here because his did.

lameness 5/5 · symmetry 54% · weight on limb 25%

DAY 40

Rehabilitation, measured

Controlled lead work, then slopes, then water. We re-score the gait every fortnight, because an owner cannot see a ten-percent gain and a number can. The twelve weeks after the operation decide how he walks at eleven.

lameness 2/5 · symmetry 82% · weight on limb 71%

DAY 84

The long way round

Symmetry back to ninety-four percent, and the red loop sits back on top of its partner where it belongs. The number is the evidence; the walk is the point. He takes the long way home, which is the only outcome his owner ever asked for.

He also stopped twice on the way back to sniff something, and wandered off the line to do it. We counted that as a full recovery.

lameness 0/5 · symmetry 94% · weight on limb 96%

Bracken is a composite of cases at a practice that does not exist. The scale is real: lameness is scored 0–5 in clinics every day.

What we do

Six things, done properly.

Consulting

Twenty-five minute appointments as standard. The extra ten minutes is where the history actually comes out.

Orthopaedics & gait

Cruciate, patella, hip and elbow work — with the rehabilitation planned before the surgery is booked.

Dentistry

Every dental under full anaesthetic, with radiographs. Scaling a tooth you have not imaged is guesswork.

Imaging

Digital radiography and ultrasound on site, so a same-day answer is usually a real option.

Senior care

Six-monthly reviews from nine years old, weighted toward mobility and pain — which is what actually shortens a good old age.

End of life

At home where you want that, unhurried, and with the vet who has known them. We do not book anything after it.

The yard

There is no waiting room.

A corridor of chairs is where a dog learns to dread the door. Ours is a walled garden with an arch straight through to the green — you wait outside on the grass, and we come and find you.

For cats there is a separate room off the far side of the yard, which never has a dog in it and never smells of one. It gets the afternoon sun, and it is usually the hardest room in the building to get anyone to leave.

The cat room

One big window, and no dogs.

Cats do not find a waiting room stressful because it is busy. They find it stressful because it smells of dogs. So theirs is a separate room on the quiet side, with one wide pane onto the garden and somewhere high to sit.

Carriers go on the floor, not the counter, and stay covered until we are ready. Small things, and they are most of the difference.

Who you will see

The same vet, each time.

Continuity is not a nicety in this work — it is most of the diagnosis. The vet who saw your dog eighteen months ago is the one who notices the quarter-inch he has stopped putting down.

Whitethorn is a two-vet practice and it will stay one. Every patient is assigned to a named vet, and if she is away, the person you see has read the whole file rather than the last line of it.

The name

Whitethorn is hawthorn.

It is the tree in every hedge along the lanes here — the one that marks where one field stops and the next begins, and that grows over gates until nobody can remember which way they opened.

It is also, and we did not plan this, an old remedy for the heart. The one red on this page is borrowed from its berry, and we use it for nothing except a reading we have actually taken.
