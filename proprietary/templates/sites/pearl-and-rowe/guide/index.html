How this page was made

Pearl & Rowe is a fictional demonstration business built as a portfolio template. All imagery is AI-generated; the dental chart, records, appointment times, and prices are sample data. No real patients, practitioners, or clinics are depicted.

Design method

The build began with a look-first sweep of the dental genre. Its default — a camera-facing whitened-smile close-up on teal-and-white clinical dressing, doctor-headshot grids, "Your Perfect Smile" — appeared more than twenty times across two searches and was refused entirely: a smile aimed at the lens reads as advertising, while absorbed care reads as trust. The keeper register came from boutique dental-suite interiors (oak, linen, sage, terracotta, arched openings, morning light) and from one outlier design that used a drawn tooth-row diagram as its centrepiece — the observation that dentistry's honest visual instrument is the chart, not the smile.

Design axes: soft-organic primary (arches, linen texture, unhurried pacing, warm paper) with data-instrument secondary (the FDI chart, the timed visit, menu pricing). The interactive chart uses real FDI notation; tooth 53 is a genuine dental situation — a retained deciduous canine — kept as the page's one deliberate wink ("still here, still counts"). The chart is served as complete static HTML; JavaScript only adds the record panel and the scroll-driven check-up pass, so readers without scripts get the full record.

Iteration passes — real catches

  • The chart container carried role="img" while holding 32 interactive buttons — assistive tech would have hidden them all; replaced with a described group.
  • The chart was originally built by JavaScript, which would have served script-free readers an empty card — rebuilt as static HTML with JS as pure enhancement.
  • Tooth tap targets compress below comfortable size on phones, so the five written records now stay visible on narrow screens instead of hiding behind taps.
  • A double-typed character from an edit rendered as a stray ">" glyph inside the chart card; caught on the second render pass.
  • Ambition-lane additions: the FDI quadrant midline gained its anatomical breathing space, and the completed check-up now surfaces tooth 53's record on its own — the wink introduces itself.

The founder fix round — same day

Founder review returned four notes, all applied within the hour:

  • "The teeth look like cinema seats." Fair. The chart's crowns were redrawn with real anatomy (cusped molars, pointed canines, chiselled incisors), the two rows curved into converging dental arches meeting at the midline, lower teeth flipped bite-up, and a soft gum band now sits behind each arch. It reads as a mouth.
  • The mirror reflection read as creepy — the hygienist appeared to be "holding a face." The scene was reminted entirely on the founder's casting direction: an elderly silver-bearded dentist in round glasses, seated at eye level, explaining with open hands and a warm smile. No mirror. One take. (One blurred bottle at the frame's far edge carries illegible label traces — disclosed here, unreadable at any real size.)
  • "Still a bit wordy." Around eighty words cut across the promises, timeline, studio list, and close.
  • "Add nature — bright, sunny, outdoorsy." Dentistry's mental image is being cooped up indoors, so the page now answers it: a hedge-enclosed garden band right after the chart (the studio's courtyard, where waiting happens) and a walk-out finale — arms spread down a tree-lined path — above the close. Garden take 1 was rejected for pseudo-signage on a building facade; walk take 1 for pseudo-lettering on a white jacket back. Both keepers recomposed the printable surface out of frame (hedges instead of buildings; a knit that takes no print).

Fix-round spend: 0.60 credits across five takes (three keepers, two rejects). Site total: 1.32 credits, eleven takes, six keepers — every keeper pinned with provenance.

Type & fonts

Literata (variable, with italics) for display and editorial moments; Atkinson Hyperlegible — designed by the Braille Institute for low-vision legibility — for everything read at small sizes, which is the care register expressed typographically. Both OFL, vendored locally; the page makes no external requests.
