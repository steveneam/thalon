How this page was made

Marl & Cane is a fictional estate. It does not exist, the wine does not exist, and nobody can visit the cellar door. The page is a demonstration of a build method, and everything on it that looks like a claim about a real business is invented for that purpose.

The picture is generated, and it is one continuous take

Every image on this site is AI-generated. None of it is a photograph of a real vineyard, and no third-party or stock imagery was used.

The scroll sequence — the bunch changing colour — is 81 stills taken from a single eight-second generated video clip, not 81 separate images. Two keyframes were generated first: the bunch unripe, and the same bunch with the fruit turned. One clip was then generated between them, and the page scrubs its frames against your scroll position. That is why the berries stay in exactly the same places while their colour changes: they are the same shot, not a slideshow of near-misses.

What was measured rather than guessed

  • The take was two-thirds useful. The clip runs 193 frames; the fruit stops changing at about frame 126, and everything after that is one still picture. Sampling evenly across the whole clip would have spent a third of this page's scroll on a frozen image, so the page ships only the live range. The range is recorded in the asset manifest, not trimmed by hand.
  • 81 frames, not 41 or 121. Frame count was chosen by measuring how much changes between neighbouring frames and comparing it against sequences on two other sites that already read smoothly.
  • Each chapter's length is derived from the frames it drives rather than from how long its paragraph happened to be. Driven in a real browser and counted frame by frame, that works out at 40–60 pixels of scroll per frame on a desktop (median 50) and 35–60 on a phone (median 45), with no frame starved of scroll and none unreachable.
  • Exposure is levelled frame by frame. Generated clips drift in brightness over their first second. Each frame is corrected onto one measured reference patch of the background, so the picture does not quietly warm up as you scroll.

The colour of the page

The page is deliberately achromatic — bone and ink — and holds exactly one colour, which moves as you scroll. Its hue is measured off the frames themselves: the lit fruit reads about 68° (yellow-green) at the start, 40° as it turns, and 4° (red) as it darkens.

Two honest caveats. The lightness of that accent is not taken from the photograph — it is solved per hue so the colour keeps a constant contrast ratio against the background (never below 4.8:1), because a fixed lightness across a hue sweep fails legibility at the green end: measured, 29 of the 81 frames failed it before that fix. And the closing red-violet is a design choice, not a measurement: by the last frames the fruit is too desaturated for a hue to mean anything, so that one number was chosen rather than read.

The ripening chart

The sugar and acid readings are invented data for a fictional vintage, chosen to be plausible for Cabernet Sauvignon. They are not measurements from a real block.

The chart is drawn as real markup from a single block of numbers embedded in the page, and the script that animates it reads the same block. With JavaScript switched off you still get the whole chart, the whole table, every chapter and one frame of the sequence — nothing here is drawn by script that isn't already in the page.

The models named are the ones that actually ran, which is not always the one requested — this vendor sometimes substitutes a faster variant. Ten stills were generated and five kept. The harvest photograph carried a small artefact near its lower edge; rather than retouch it, the published crop composes it out of frame, and that crop is part of the recorded recipe.

The one thing that is hand-written

The pencilled note under the chart — 22 Mar · 14 mm — held off four days — is the page's single deliberate informality, and the reason the picking date is the 26th and not the 22nd. Growers really do annotate their lab books like that.
