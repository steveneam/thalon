Chapter one

Marl, and cane

The soil is marl — limestone clay, pale and cold, and slow to give anything up. It holds water in a dry February and drains in a wet one. We cane-prune, which means every winter we cut the vine back to one year-old shoot and start again.

Neither of these things changed this year. They are why the estate is called what it is: they are the part that stays put.

Chapter two

Véraison

The word for the turn. Until now the berry has been a hard green thing that does nothing but grow — no sugar to speak of, and acid enough to strip your teeth. Then, over about a fortnight, it stops growing and starts ripening.

It does not happen to the bunch. It happens to each berry, one at a time, in no order anyone has ever been able to predict. For a couple of weeks a single bunch carries green, rose, red and near-black fruit at once, and that is the only time of year it looks like this.

Chapter three

What the colour is

The colour is not decoration and it is not a ripeness gauge you can drink from. It is anthocyanin arriving in the skin, and it turns up on roughly the same clock as the sugar — which is why a grower can stand in a row and know, near enough, where the block is.

Near enough is not a number, though. For the number you pick fruit and crush it, every week, and write it down.

Chapter four

And then it stops

By the end of February the fruit is as dark as it is going to get. The picture stops changing — this one genuinely does; there is nothing more to watch.

Everything that decides the vintage happens in the five weeks after that, and none of it is visible. So the rest of this page is the laboratory book.

One 200-berry sample from the same twelve vines, every Monday at seven in the morning. The window opens when sugar clears 23.5° and acid is still above 5.8 g/L — after that the acid goes before the sugar stops, and you are making a different wine than you meant to.

The half that does not change

Cold soil buys you time

Marl warms slowly and holds its water deep, so the vine is never in a hurry. In a hot year that is worth more than anything we could do in the winery: the sugar climbs at a walk instead of a run, and the acid is still there when the flavour arrives.

It is also why our picking window tends to open a fortnight after everyone else's, which has caused arguments.

The decision
26 March

The window opened on the 17th. We would have picked on the 22nd, and then 14 millimetres fell overnight on the 21st. Fruit picked wet is fruit picked dilute, so we waited four days for the block to dry and gave up two tenths of acid doing it.

That is the whole vintage, really. One decision, made on a weather forecast, that cannot be made again — and the reason this label will never taste the way it did in 2023.

What is in the bottle

One wine, 2,900 bottles

Whole bunches into concrete, wild ferment, eighteen months in old oak and no fining. It is dark, dusty and firm for the first two years and then it lets go.

Cellar door open Friday to Sunday. There is no mailing list and no allocation — when it is gone, we wait a year like everyone else.
